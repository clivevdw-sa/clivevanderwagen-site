Why I wrote it

I've spent the better part of two decades in boardrooms watching leadership teams spend real money on culture work that had no chance of working, and nobody in the room willing to say why.

Thriving Culture is that conversation. It argues that most culture problems get treated like a poster problem: fix the values, run the survey, tidy the comms. The real issue is whether people's basic psychological needs are actually being met in the room, every day, by the people leading them.

It draws on Transactional Analysis, the Enneagram, Functional Fluency, the Drama Triangle and the Empowerment Dynamic, and Daniel Kim's Core Cycle of Success, along with case work inside some of South Africa's largest financial services and hospitality brands, including a full customer experience case study.

No frameworks for their own sake. No motivational gloss. Just what I've seen work.

Chapter 1

Culture is not a poster on the wall

There is a particular kind of quiet that settles over an organisation when something has gone wrong with its culture. It is not the loud, obvious kind of wrong. No one is shouting, no one has quit in a dramatic scene. It is something subtler. You feel it when you walk into the building. You sense it in how people answer the phone, in the length of the pause before someone responds to a question, in the way eyes drop when a leader enters the room. Culture has a texture. It has a smell. And once you learn to read it, you cannot stop noticing it.

I have been walking into organisations for years. Call centres, banks, hotels, government departments, small businesses with five people and big ones with thousands, and what strikes me every time is how much is communicated before a single training session begins. The receptionist who barely looks up or ever smiles. The call centre agent who answers with a script but no warmth. The way the L&D team briefs me with their eyes slightly downcast, as if they are embarrassed about what I am about to find. Culture announces itself. The question is whether you are paying attention.

The Poster Problem

For a while, I worked for an employee experience agency. Our job was to help organisations communicate change, and most of that change was cultural. We would be brought in by consultants who had been paid handsomely to design a new set of values, a refreshed purpose statement, and a culture framework. They had done the workshops with leadership. They had the slides. They had the words.

Then they needed someone to communicate all of this to the people who actually worked there.

And so it went up on the walls. In the lifts. In the boardrooms. Beautiful design. Carefully chosen words. Respect. Innovation. Customer First. Integrity. The kind of language that costs a lot of money to arrive at and means almost nothing once it is printed, laminated, and posted on the wall.

I do not say this to be cynical. The people who commissioned those posters genuinely wanted change. But they had confused the declaration of culture with the creation of it. They had confused the map with the territory.

“Culture is not what you say you stand for. It is what you actually do, especially when no one is watching, especially when it is inconvenient, especially when it costs you something.”

The real definition of culture is both simpler and more demanding than any values statement. Culture is the way we do things around here. Not the way we say we do things. Not the way we aspire to do things. The way we actually do them, today, in this meeting, in this conversation, in the decision made at 16:45 on a Friday, when everyone is tired, and no one is documenting it.

What made this pattern so visible to me was not an abstract observation. It was a person.
