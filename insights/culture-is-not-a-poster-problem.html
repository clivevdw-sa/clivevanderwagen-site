Culture is not a poster problem

Values workshops and engagement surveys describe a culture. They don't change one. What changes it is whether people's basic psychological needs are met, every day, by the people leading them.

15 September 2026 · Updated 27 October 2026, 4 min read
Quick answer

Organisational culture changes when people's basic psychological needs, for recognition, structure and stimulation, are met every day by the people leading them. Values workshops, engagement surveys and refreshed comms describe a culture. They don't change it. Culture is the sum of thousands of small interactions, which makes it a leadership problem rather than a communications one. That's good news, because leadership can be developed.

I've spent the better part of two decades in boardrooms watching leadership teams spend real money on culture work that had no chance of working. The values get refreshed. The survey gets run. The comms get tidied up. Then everyone waits for the culture to change.

It doesn't, because none of that touches the thing culture is made of.

What is organisational culture actually made of?

A culture is the sum of thousands of small interactions. A manager who looks up when someone walks in, or doesn't. A meeting where disagreement is welcome, or quietly punished. Feedback that arrives as information, or as a verdict.

Eric Berne, who developed Transactional Analysis, described basic human hungers: for recognition, for structure, for stimulation. When those are met at work, people bring their best thinking. When they aren't, people go looking for them in other ways, and that's where you get politics, drama and disengagement.

Who actually changes a culture?

This is why I don't believe in culture change as a separate project. The culture is delivered by leaders, every day, in how they meet or ignore those needs. A beautifully worded values statement can't recognise someone. A leader can.

So the useful questions aren't about the poster. They're about the room. Do people know what's expected of them? Do they feel seen for the work they do? Are they stretched, or just busy? Can they say the uncomfortable thing without it costing them?

No frameworks for their own sake. The test of culture work is whether it changes what happens in the room on a Tuesday afternoon.

This is the argument I make at length in Thriving Culture. The short version: stop treating culture as a communications problem. It's a leadership problem, and that's good news, because leadership is something you can actually develop.

Four questions that show whether your culture is working

  • Do people know what's expected of them?
  • Do they feel seen for the work they do?
  • Are they stretched, or just busy?
  • Can they say the uncomfortable thing without it costing them?
