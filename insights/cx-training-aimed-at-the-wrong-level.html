Your customer experience training didn't fail. It aimed at the wrong level.

Scripts and service standards change what people say for a few weeks. If you want the change to stick, you have to work on what people believe about the customer, and about themselves.

1 October 2026 · Updated 28 October 2026, 5 min read
Quick answer

Most customer experience training isn't sustained on the floor because it targets behaviour (scripts, phrases, service standards) and leaves untouched the mental models that drive it. Under pressure, people don't reach for the training. They reach for what they believe about the customer and about themselves. Training that works on those beliefs, on ego states and on ownership is what holds. On one financial services rollout with more than 320 staff, it moved Net Effort Score by 35 points.

What did a call centre teach me about customer experience?

I worked in a call centre. I sold advertising space in magazines and spent my days phoning business owners and marketing managers, convincing them that our magazine would do wonders for their brand.

We had a script. And a list of responses to objections.

They trained us to say the right things, but never to look at how the customer was left feeling.

Cancellations were high. And many customers never advertised a second time.

Because we were robots (the bad robots, not the fancy new AI robots). Most of us. We hated working there. The staff churn was huge. We could potentially make good money, but I felt like I was selling my soul rather than space.

(I wrote more about that call centre, including the day they took away my chair, in What are you hungry for?)

But I learnt so much from that experience, and I'm deeply grateful for it. I learnt the power of talking to people. I realised I made a sale when I listened to the customer rather than pitching them. I learnt that customers needed to feel safe, especially when purchasing.

It has shaped how I train call centre agents today. I love training them because they are often under-invested in. They spend their working days being told what to do and often feel like they have no autonomy.

That's why I do customer experience training differently.

Most customer experience training I'm asked to look at has the same shape. A set of service standards. Some role-play. A script for difficult calls. A certificate at the end. Things feel good after the training, and three months later, the scores look exactly like they did before.

When that happens, the usual conclusion is that the training wasn't good enough, so the business sticks to product training.

My conclusion is different.

The training was aimed at the wrong level.

Why doesn't customer experience training stick?

What a customer experiences is behavioural. The tone of voice, the empathy or lack thereof, whether someone takes ownership or passes the problem along. So it makes sense that training targets behaviour. Teach the phrases (“Apologies for the inconvenience”), model the steps, test the recall.

The problem is that psychologically, behaviour is an output. It's what people do with what they believe. If an agent believes the customer is the enemy, or that nobody above them has their back, or that any mistake will be punished, no script survives contact with a difficult call.

Under pressure, people don't reach for the training. They reach for their mental model.

What should CX training work on instead?

In Transactional Analysis terms, a lot of poor service comes from people operating out of an Adapted Child (submissive, overly apologetic, anxious) or a Critical Parent ego state (bossy, dominating) rather than the Adult: logical, rational and choosing to respond to the customer rather than reacting to them. You can't script someone into the Adult. You can help them notice what pulls them out of it, and practise getting back.

That's the work I do with service teams. We spend less time on what to say and more time on what's happening inside the person saying it. What assumptions they carry about customers (customers don't read; brokers are stupid), the needs that go unmet in their own working day, and the moments where those two collide.

What results does this approach get?

On a recent rollout with more than 320 frontline staff in financial services, we didn't hand anyone a new script. We worked on mental models, ego states and ownership. As a result, the client's Net Effort Score improved by 35 points. (Net Effort Score measures how easy customers find it to get what they need, so a higher score means less effort for the customer.) Complaints went down and compliments went up. And since then, this has been sustained. Delegates rated the programme between 4.88 and 4.96 out of 5 across every measure, and 100% said it was worthwhile.

That's not because the people became nicer. It's because they stopped fighting the customer and started working with them, and that showed up in how hard customers had to work to get what they needed.

If your CX training only changes what people say, expect it to last about as long as people remember the words.

What does a customer actually need from an agent?

Customer experience training has to start with digging below the surface to see what causes a representative to react. What is causing them to rattle off platitudes rather than demonstrating ownership.

Behavioural training pushes representatives into Adapted Child, and often a customer needs an agent in the Structuring Parent ego state, as Functional Fluency describes it. This means they speak with authority, guide the customer, let them know they are in safe hands, and are clear about next steps.

Training people what to say or to speak with a smile in their voice is not sustainable.

We need to train them to control what they can control. Their thinking. Their responses. Their attitudes. And their personal decision to be the best at their job and get great results for the business. One call at a time.

Before you buy your next programme, ask one question: is this designed to change behaviour, or to change what drives it? Only one of those holds when the call gets hard.

What should you ask before buying CX training?

  • Is it designed to change behaviour, or to change what drives it?
  • Does it deal with what staff believe about customers, and about their own role?
  • Does it help people notice when they slip out of the Adult ego state under pressure, and get back?
  • Will it be measured against your CX metrics, such as Net Effort Score, and not just training scores?
