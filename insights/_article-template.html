Your customer experience training didn't fail. It aimed at the wrong level.

Scripts and service standards change what people say for a few weeks. If you want the change to hold, you have to work on what people believe about the customer, and about themselves.

1 October 2026, 5 min read
Quick answer

Most customer experience training fails because it targets behaviour (scripts, phrases, service standards) and leaves untouched the mental models that drive it. Under pressure, people don't reach for the training. They reach for what they believe about the customer and about themselves. Training that works on those beliefs, on ego states and on ownership is what holds. On one financial services rollout with more than 320 staff, it moved Net Effort Score by 35 points.

Most customer experience training I'm asked to look at has the same shape. A set of service standards. Some role-play. A script for difficult calls. A certificate at the end. And three months later, the scores look exactly like they did before.

When that happens, the usual conclusion is that the training wasn't good enough, so the business buys more of it. My conclusion is different. The training was aimed at the wrong level.

Why doesn't customer experience training stick?

What a customer experiences is behaviour: the tone of voice, the patience, whether someone takes ownership or passes the problem along. So it makes sense that training targets behaviour. Teach the phrases, model the steps, test the recall.

The problem is that behaviour is an output. It's what people do with what they believe. If an agent believes the customer is the enemy, or that nobody above them has their back, or that any mistake will be punished, no script survives contact with a difficult call. Under pressure, people don't reach for the training. They reach for their mental model.

What should CX training work on instead?

In Transactional Analysis terms, a lot of poor service comes from people operating out of a defensive Child or a critical Parent ego state rather than the Adult: reacting to the customer instead of responding to them. You can't script someone into the Adult. You can help them notice what pulls them out of it, and practise getting back.

That's the work I do with service teams. We spend less time on what to say and more time on what's happening inside the person saying it: the assumptions they carry about customers, the needs that go unmet in their own working day, and the moments where those two collide.

What results does this approach get?

On a recent rollout with more than 320 frontline staff in financial services, we didn't hand anyone a new script. We worked on mental models, ego states and ownership. The client's Net Effort Score improved by 35 points. Complaints went down and compliments went up.

That's not because the people became nicer. It's because they stopped fighting the customer and started working with them, and that showed up in how hard customers had to work to get what they needed.

If your CX training only changes what people say, expect it to last about as long as people remember the words.

Before you buy your next programme, ask one question: is this designed to change behaviour, or to change what drives it? Only one of those holds when the call gets hard.

What should you ask before buying CX training?

  • Is it designed to change behaviour, or to change what drives it?
  • Does it deal with what staff believe about customers, and about their own role?
  • Does it help people notice when they slip out of the Adult ego state under pressure, and get back?
  • Will it be measured against your CX metrics, such as Net Effort Score, and not just training scores?
